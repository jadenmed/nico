About Me

My name is Nicholas Mazorra, and I am a Finance major in the Herbert Wertheim College of Business at Florida State University. This ePortfolio was created for ENC 2135: Research, Genre, and Context, and it collects the major writing I completed in the course.

The main project in this portfolio, Scrolling Into Debt: How Social Media Influences the Spending Habits of Young People, looks at why young people end up making unplanned purchases from social media, using research on both consumer psychology and financial literacy. The idea actually came from my own experience collecting soccer jerseys and buying limited-release kits I saw advertised on Instagram.

This site includes a supporting artifact from an earlier draft of the project, the final globally revised version of the project, and a reflective statement on what I learned about writing, research, and genre this semester.

This was my first attempt at the proposal for "Scrolling Into Debt." The research questions were already in place, and all six sources were already annotated, but the writing was general — for example, the problem was described simply as "young people spend hours daily on Instagram and TikTok surrounded by ads that do not look like ads." Each annotation stated a finding on its own, without connecting it to the other sources or to my own experience.

Artifact 2: Research Question & Angle Brainstorm

Completed: Before the first draft, as prewriting for the proposal

Before I wrote the first draft, I jotted down a working note to figure out what angle the project should actually take. At that point I only had a general interest in "social media and spending" and a personal habit I wanted to explain: buying soccer jerseys the moment a limited drop showed up on Instagram. The note below is where I narrowed that down into the three research questions that carried through both drafts, and where I first separated the project into two halves — the psychology of why people buy, and the financial literacy side of why some people are more vulnerable to that pull than others.

Working note: "Why do I buy jerseys I didn't plan to buy? It's not about the jersey, it's the countdown / the 'only 50 left.' That's not just me, it's probably every young person on these apps. Two things going on: (1) how social media gets people to buy, (2) why some people can resist it and others can't — that's probably a money knowledge thing, not just a willpower thing. Need sources on both sides and something that connects them."

I'm including this note because it's the moment the project stopped being a school assignment about "social media" in general and became an argument with a real structure — one I could then go find sources for. The two halves in this note became the two threads (identification/ comparison research and financial-literacy research) that the annotated bibliography and, later, the Synthesis section were built around.

Cover Page — Global Changes

Three global (structural) changes I made between the original draft and this revision:

  • Added a personal narrative. The original draft described the problem in general terms. The revision opens with my own experience collecting soccer jerseys and being pulled into unplanned purchases by countdown-timer posts, and connects that example back through the whole bibliography.
  • Added a Significance of the Study section. The first draft moved straight from the research questions into the literature review with no explanation of why the project mattered. The revision adds a section explaining that financial literacy research treats spending as a calculation while marketing research treats consumers as psychological targets, and that this project sits between the two.
  • Rewrote the annotations and added a Synthesis section. In the original, each annotation was a short summary of a finding. In the revision, every annotation includes the study's method and sample size, connects the finding to my jersey example, and the bibliography closes with a new Synthesis section tying all six sources back to the three research questions.

Reflective Statement

Genre & Genre Theory

Going into this project, I thought of a "research proposal" as just a formal way of saying "here's my topic and here's my sources." Writing one taught me that it's actually its own genre with its own moves: it has to introduce a problem, justify why the problem matters (the Significance of the Study section), ask specific research questions, and then show, through the annotated bibliography, that existing research can actually answer those questions. An annotated bibliography is its own genre inside that genre — it isn't a summary of a source, it's an argument about what the source is for. Once I understood that, I stopped writing annotations that just restated a finding and started writing ones that explained why that specific study belonged next to the others in my bibliography.

Composing Process

My first draft had all six sources annotated and all three research questions written, but it read like a list. Each annotation reported a finding and stopped there — "Croes and Bartels found social identification with influencers is the main driver of buying" — without connecting it to anything else. When I went back to revise, I compared that draft against my own prewriting note (Artifact 2) and realized the project had lost the thing that made me care about it in the first place: my own jersey-buying habit. So the biggest change in my composing process was working backward from that personal example into the research, instead of writing the research first and trying to bolt an example on at the end. That's also when I added the Significance of the Study section and, at the very end, the Synthesis section — both came from asking "so what does all this add up to?" instead of just "what did each source say?"

Rhetorical Situation

My audience for this proposal is an academic reader (my professor) evaluating whether my research questions are worth pursuing and whether my sources can answer them. That could have meant writing in a completely impersonal, clinical voice. Instead, I opened the revised draft with the story about my soccer jersey collection. I made that choice because the purpose of a proposal isn't just to list facts, it's to convince a reader the project matters — and a concrete, specific example of the exact behavior I'm studying does more work than another general sentence about "young people and social media." The personal example also let me show, rather than just claim, that I understood the mechanism (identification with a brand or club) the academic sources were describing.

Research & Sources

My bibliography mixes peer-reviewed studies (Croes & Bartels; Phua, Jin, & Kim; Lusardi & Mitchell; Lusardi, Mitchell, & Curto) with an institutional report (the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau) and a nonpartisan research organization (Pew Research Center). Writing this project taught me that credibility isn't one-size-fits-all — a peer-reviewed journal article is credible because of its methodology and the fact that other researchers reviewed it, while the CFPB report is credible because it comes from the federal agency with direct authority over consumer finance, and Pew is credible because of its sample size and its reputation for nonpartisan survey methodology. Learning to say why each source was credible, in its own terms, made the annotations stronger than if I had just called everything "reliable." Writing the Synthesis section was the biggest shift in how I used my sources: instead of treating my six sources as six separate findings, I had to explicitly connect them — showing that the identification research (Croes & Bartels; Phua, Jin, & Kim) explains the spending behavior, the financial literacy research (Lusardi & Mitchell; Lusardi, Mitchell, & Curto) explains why some people are more vulnerable to it than others, and the CFPB and Pew sources tie both of those back to the real-world scale of the problem. That synthesis is the difference between an annotated bibliography and an actual argument, and it's the single biggest thing I learned about research writing in this course.
