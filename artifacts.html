Composing Process

Artifacts

Three pieces that take the same research and translate it into three completely different genres — for three completely different audiences.

Why These Are Included

Same Research, Three Audiences

Each artifact below takes my underlying research on social media and spending among college students ages 18–29 and reshapes it for a different reader — a peer scrolling a feed, a parent in the middle of a conversation, and a platform with no personal stake in the argument at all. Building all three taught me that adapting research to a genre and an audience is its own skill, separate from the research itself.

Artifact 1: Instagram Post Design

Audience: college students ages 18–29, scrolling their own feeds

I designed this piece to look like a real Instagram post, with a profile icon, engagement icons, and a caption, rather than a generic graphic, because my audience for this genre was college students ages 18 to 29 scrolling their own feeds. Before choosing a black-and-cream color palette, I researched color psychology in marketing, since a post that visually resembled an advertisement would get scrolled past exactly like the posts it was trying to interrupt.

Building this artifact taught me that persuasion isn't only about what you say, it's about whether the format itself earns the right to be read in the first place, and it pushed me to design in a genre I had never worked in before. This project showed me that translating academic research into a genuinely native, audience-appropriate visual format is its own distinct skill, separate from writing the research itself.

Artifact 2: Parent & Educator Infographic

Audience: parents and educators, mid-conversation with a young adult

This infographic translated the same underlying research into five color-coded, numbered reframes for parents and educators, an audience that needed something usable in the middle of an actual conversation with a young adult, not a resource to sit down and study in advance. Building it taught me how much a single research finding can change shape depending on who needs to use it and when, since the same evidence that filled paragraphs in my essay had to become a sentence a parent could say out loud without rehearsing it first.

This artifact helped me practice a kind of rhetorical compression I hadn't done before in this course, cutting an academic argument down to its essentials without losing what made it true. It showed me that simplifying an argument for a new audience is not the same as weakening it; done well, it's its own kind of rhetorical skill.

Artifact 3: Open Letter to Social Media Platforms

Audience: product and trust-&-safety teams at platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat

This letter was addressed to the product and trust-and-safety teams at platforms like Instagram, TikTok, and Snapchat, asking them to add a simple, optional pause before checkout for younger users. Writing it pushed me to think rhetorically for an audience with zero personal stake in my argument, a very different challenge than writing for students or parents, since I had to anticipate the exact objection a platform would raise — that added friction hurts conversion — and answer it before they could raise it themselves.

This artifact taught me that persuading someone with no built-in incentive to agree with you requires leading with evidence and feasibility rather than emotion. It also showed me that naming and directly addressing the strongest counterargument up front makes a piece of writing more credible, not weaker, a lesson I now apply well beyond this one genre.
